People ask how many years a fence will last, and any honest answer starts with "it depends." Two fences built the same week can age very differently depending on the lot. Here's what makes the difference in our area.

## Posts decide most of it

Most wood fences fail at the posts, right at the ground, where wood stays wet and decay and termites do their work. The humid Gulf South is hard on wood in the ground. In a USDA Forest Products Laboratory test in southern Mississippi, a site with high decay and termite hazard, [untreated southern pine posts lasted only two years](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/25196), while posts treated with the commercial preservatives in the test were projected to last for decades.

The takeaways:

- **Treatment matters** for any wood post set in the ground.
- **Steel posts** sidestep rot at the ground line. Our [steel vs. wood posts guide](/resources/steel-vs-wood-fence-posts/) compares them.
- **The hole matters.** We never pour concrete into standing water. We get the water out, add gravel, and then set the post.

## Shade and water

Under the heavy tree cover in The Woodlands and Kingwood, fences stay damp longer, and mildew, algae, and pollen settle into the grain. Low spots where water sits against the bottom of the fence wear out first. A rot board along the bottom can be replaced without touching the pickets.

## Maintenance

Stain doesn't make a fence last forever, but it slows graying and mildew and helps the boards shed water. Cleaning and restaining on a regular schedule keeps the pickets in better shape. See [staining and power washing](/fence-staining-power-washing/).

Other things that shorten a fence's life:

- **Sprinklers** that soak the same boards every day.
- **Soil, mulch, or plant beds** piled against the bottom boards.
- **Vines and shrubs** growing into the fence.
- **Gates** left to sag, which strains the gate posts.

## Repair or replace?

Sound posts with a few broken pickets or a loose rail are worth repairing. Once posts are failing along much of the line, each repair holds for less time, and replacing the run is usually the better use of money. In The Woodlands, replacing more than eight linear feet needs approval either way. See our [repair approval guide](/resources/woodlands-fence-repair-approval/).
