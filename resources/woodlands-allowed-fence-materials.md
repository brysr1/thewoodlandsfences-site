When you plan a fence in The Woodlands, the material decision is made for you more than you might expect. Section 2.6 of the Township's [Residential Development Standards](https://www.thewoodlandstownship-tx.gov/files/sharedassets/main/v/2/covenant-administration/documents/2024-residential-standards-recorded-12-10-2024.pdf) lists four allowed materials and a longer list of prohibited ones. Here's how that plays out on real projects.

## The allowed list

Fences in The Woodlands may be built of:

- **Wood**, which in practice usually means cedar privacy, picket, or split rail.
- **Metal**, such as ornamental steel or wrought iron.
- **Stone.**
- **Masonry.**

The standards also require fences to be built of materials that keep them **straight and plumb**. A fence that leans or sags isn't just a maintenance issue here. It's a standards issue.

## What's not allowed

The prohibited list in Section 2.6 includes:

- **Chain link, wire, or wire mesh**, unless it's fully screened from view inside an approved solid fence.
- **Sheet metal or expanded metal.**
- **Stamped metal posts.**
- **Plastic or fiberglass**, which rules out vinyl fencing.
- **Rope, bamboo, or reed.**
- **Wire-bound wood pickets.**
- **Unfinished cinder block.**

Section 1.6, the Township's list of materials that are unacceptable throughout the standards, adds **hog wire** and **gloss paint** to the list.

### What about composite?

Composite boards such as Trex aren't on the allowed list, and we don't build composite fences inside The Woodlands. If you're set on composite, it may work in another neighborhood with different rules. Our [composite guide](/resources/composite-fence-woodlands/) covers where it fits around north Houston and why it's off the table inside the Township.

## Steel posts on a wood fence

Many owners ask for steel posts on a wood fence. The standards allow **galvanized steel posts up to two inches in diameter**, placed on the **inside** of a solid fence and no taller than the fence. Section 1.6 says other metal posts are unacceptable unless they're part of a wrought iron fence.

In practice, that means post-in-the-ground steel works on a cedar privacy fence when the posts sit on your side and stay hidden by the pickets. Our [steel vs. wood posts guide](/resources/steel-vs-wood-fence-posts/) covers the trade-offs.

## Black mesh for pets

Black mesh is the one place where a wire-type material shows up legally, and it has limits:

- **Temporary pet barrier:** on a wrought iron fence, black mesh can be used for up to **six months**, no taller than **three feet**. If it stays longer than six months, it has to be screened with evergreen plants.
- **Interior screening:** fabric-type black mesh can go on the inside of a wrought iron fence if it's no taller than the fence, can't be seen from a street, and isn't on lake, golf course, or public water frontage.

If you need a fence that keeps a dog in for the long term, a different design usually works better than mesh. Our [fence for dogs guide](/resources/fence-for-dogs-woodlands/) covers the options.

## Choosing a material that gets approved

Within those limits, three choices fit most lots:

- **Cedar** for privacy fences on interior lots.
- **Ornamental steel** for golf course and lake lots, pool barriers, and front yards, where see-through fences are expected.
- **Masonry or stone** columns with cedar or steel panels, where they suit the house or your Neighborhood Criteria call for them.

Your village's Neighborhood Criteria can go further and require a specific design on your street. We check those before we draw anything.
