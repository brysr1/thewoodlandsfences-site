People ask us about the best time to build a fence in The Woodlands almost as often as they ask about price. The short answer: you can build one any month of the year here, and we do. But the season changes three things that matter: how much lead time you need for approval, how wet the ground is when the posts go in, and when you can stain the new wood.

Here's how each season plays out on lots in The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, and Splendora, and how we'd time a project if the calendar is up to you.

## Start with approval, not the weather

Inside The Woodlands, almost every new or replacement fence needs written approval from your village's Residential Design Review Committee before anyone digs. That step takes more calendar time than the build itself, which usually runs one to three days on site.

So the real question isn't only "when should we build?" It's "when should we apply?" If you want a fence up by a certain date, count backward and leave room for the review. Our [step-by-step approval guide](/resources/woodlands-fence-approval-rdrc/) covers what to submit and where to check current meeting schedules and deadlines. Outside the Township, most HOAs in the area have their own architectural review, so the same rule applies: get the paperwork moving first.

## The best time to build a fence, season by season

### Fall: our favorite window

From October into early December, the heat breaks, and hurricane season ends on November 30. Many hardwoods drop their leaves, so it's easier to see the fence line, the trunks, and the low limbs we need to plan around. Homeowners also have time to finish approval and build before the holidays.

If you're thinking about a fence for spring, fall is when to start. Applying now means you're not waiting in line behind everyone else who decided in March.

### Winter: workable, with a catch

Winter fences go in fine, and scheduling is often easier. The catch is the ground. On the flatter lots in this part of Montgomery County, the clay subsoil sits closer to the surface, and in wet months water perches on top of it. Post holes can slough in or fill with water before the concrete goes in.

We never pour concrete into a wet hole. We get the water out, put gravel in the bottom, and then set the post. That adds a little time on some days, and a long stretch of rain can push a start date. On the rare night it drops below freezing, we hold off on concrete until the next morning.

### Spring: the busy season

Spring is when most people decide they want a new fence, so it's the busiest stretch of the year for installers and for review committees. The ground can still be wet early in the season. Pine pollen also coats everything from late winter into spring, which matters more for staining than for building.

If spring is your target, the best move is to get your quote and application in during the winter so you're ready to build when the weather turns.

### Summer: fast drying, long days

Summer ground is usually firm and dry, and post holes hold their shape well. The tradeoffs are heat, afternoon storms, and the start of hurricane season on June 1. If a storm damages an old fence, demand for repairs jumps at the same time, so a summer project planned ahead gets a better spot on the calendar than one started after a storm.

## Think about staining when you pick a date

A new wood fence can't be stained the day it's finished. The wood has to dry first, and stain goes on best in a dry stretch of weather, not in the middle of a rainy week or under a fresh layer of pollen. A fence built in fall often dries through winter and is ready to stain in spring once the worst of the pollen settles. See our [staining and power washing page](/fence-staining-power-washing/) for which stains the Township pre-approves.

## The short version

- **Building for spring or summer?** Get your quote and approval started in fall or winter.
- **Fence failing now?** Don't wait for a season. A leaning fence only gets worse in the next storm.
- **On a low, flat lot?** Expect the wet months to add a little time for dewatering post holes, and ask any installer how they handle a hole full of water.
