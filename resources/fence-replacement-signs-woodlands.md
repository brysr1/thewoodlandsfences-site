A lot of fences in The Woodlands went in with the house, and many are now 15 to 30 years old. At some point every homeowner asks the same thing: patch it one more time, or plan a fence replacement? The answer usually depends on the posts, not the pickets, and on how many problems are showing up at once.

These are the six signs we look for when we walk a fence in The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, or Splendora. One of them alone usually means a repair. Three or more usually means it's time.

## Signs it's time for fence replacement

### 1. Posts are leaning along much of the line

One leaning post after a storm is a repair. A fence that leans in several places, or moves when you push on it at a dozen spots, has posts failing at the ground line. Wood posts rot where soil, air, and water meet, and once one goes, its neighbors are usually close behind. Resetting posts one at a time on a fence like that only buys a season.

### 2. Rot at the bottom of the fence

Push a screwdriver into the rot board, the bottom of the pickets, and the base of a few posts. If the wood is soft or crumbles, water has been sitting against it for years. On the flatter lots around The Woodlands, clay sits closer to the surface and water perches on top of it in the wet months, so the ground under a fence can stay damp from fall into spring. Rot that has reached the posts and rails, not just a few pickets, is a replacement sign.

### 3. You've already replaced several posts

If you or a previous owner have already swapped out three or four posts, look at what's left. Those posts went in at the same time and sat in the same ground. When a replacement fence goes in, this is also the moment to decide on post type. Our [steel vs. wood fence posts comparison](/resources/steel-vs-wood-fence-posts/) covers how each holds up in wet ground and wind.

### 4. Rails are sagging or pulling loose

Rails carry the pickets. When they crack, sag in the middle, or pull away from the posts, the pickets go wavy and gaps open up. Re-fastening a rail with a bracket is a quick fix if the post is solid. When the rails and posts are both tired, new brackets have nothing good to grab.

### 5. Gates that won't stay fixed

A gate that drags or won't latch is often the first sign of a post problem. Rehanging it with new hinges works when the gate post is sound. If the gate keeps sagging a few months after every fix, the post it hangs on is moving, and that post usually isn't the only one. A new fence lets us set gate posts deeper and stiffer from the start.

### 6. Storm damage across more than one section

After a strong front or a tropical storm, look at how much went down. One section knocked over by a limb can be rebuilt to match. Damage spread along a whole side usually means the posts were already weak, and the storm just found them. Hurricane season runs through November 30, so fall is a good time to fix a fence that won't make it through another one.

## Before you replace: what to plan for

- **Approval comes first.** In The Woodlands, replacing more than eight linear feet needs written approval from your village's Residential Design Review Committee, even if the new fence matches the old one. Build that time into your plans.
- **The trees stay.** Trees over six inches in diameter, measured two feet above grade, can't be removed for a fence. If your old fence was built tight against a trunk, the new line may need to jog around it.
- **Wet holes get handled.** Winter is a busy time for replacement, and it's also when post holes on this flat ground slough in or fill with water. We pump the water out and set a gravel base before any concrete goes in. We never pour into a wet hole.
- **Tear-out is part of the job.** Removing the old fence and breaking out old footings takes labor. Our [pricing guide](/pricing-guide/) explains what moves the price.

If only one or two of these signs fit your fence, a repair is probably the better money. Our [repair and maintenance page](/maintenance-repair/) covers what we fix and how we decide.
