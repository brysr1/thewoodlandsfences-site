Lake Houston borders Kingwood, Atascocita, and Huffman, and plenty of lots back right up to it. A fence on one of those lots is a different project from a backyard privacy fence. It has to respect the view, hold up when the water comes up, and fit rules from more than one place.

## Start with where your lot ends

Before anything else, find your property line along the shore. Your survey shows it. Waterfront lines aren't always where the lawn seems to end, and a fence built past your line is a problem no matter how good it looks. Our [property survey guide](/faq/property-survey/) covers what to look for.

## Know who regulates the lake

The City of Houston has its own rules for Lake Houston. The city's [Lake Houston program](https://www.houstonpermittingcenter.org/building-code-enforcement/lake-houston) issues permits for building or making major repairs to **piers** and **bulkheads** on or in the lake, and for **dredging** in the lake's control area. The Lake Houston office is at 22627 W. Shorewood Loop Dr. in Huffman, and its number is 832-395-6620.

A typical yard fence on your own lot isn't on that list. But if a fence ties into a bulkhead, crosses toward the water, or needs work along the shoreline, call that office before you plan it.

The rest of the permit picture depends on where you are:

- **Kingwood and other addresses inside Houston city limits:** a fence permit is needed only for fences over 8 feet or masonry and concrete fences, per the [Houston Permitting Center](https://www.houstonpermittingcenter.org/hpwcode1145).
- **Unincorporated Harris County,** including much of Atascocita and Huffman: a residential fence needs a county permit only inside the special flood hazard area. See the Harris County Engineering [permit FAQ](https://oce.harriscountytx.gov/Services/Permits/FAQ).
- **Your HOA or deed restrictions:** these often matter most on the water. Some lakefront communities limit solid fences on lake lots to protect neighbors' views.

## Plan for high water

Lake Houston rises in heavy rain, and the city sometimes lowers the lake ahead of a big storm. In October 2025, for example, Houston Public Works [lowered it by 12 inches](https://www.houstonpublicworks.org/property-owners-should-prepare-for-lower-lake-levels-october-24-2025) to prepare for forecast rain and asked shoreline owners to secure their property.

For a fence, that means:

- **Check the flood map first.** We look up the lot's zone on the [FEMA Flood Map Service Center](https://msc.fema.gov/portal/home). It decides whether a county permit is needed and how the fence should be built.
- **Let water through.** An open fence lets water and debris pass. A solid fence across low ground acts like a dam and can be pushed over.
- **Set posts for wet ground.** We never pour concrete into a hole full of water. We get the water out, add gravel, and then set the post.

Our [flood zone and easement guide](/faq/flood-zone-easement-fencing/) covers the permit side in more detail.

## Choose a material that suits the water

- **Aluminum** can't rust, so it's a good fit near water. See [aluminum fences](/materials/aluminum/).
- **Powder-coated steel** is heavier and stiffer, so it's the stronger choice for gates and spots that take force. See [ornamental steel](/materials/ornamental-steel-aluminum/).
- **Cedar** works on the side and street lines, away from the water, where privacy matters more than the view.

A common layout on a lake lot is cedar privacy on the sides near the house, stepping down to an open metal fence toward the water. We check your deed restrictions before suggesting it.
