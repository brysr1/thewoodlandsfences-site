A pool fence in The Woodlands has two jobs. It has to keep small children out of the water, and it has to pass the Township's review and a code inspection. Most of the rules come from Section 2.13 of The Woodlands Township's [Residential Development Standards](https://www.thewoodlandstownship-tx.gov/files/sharedassets/main/v/2/covenant-administration/documents/2024-residential-standards-recorded-12-10-2024.pdf), with fence-specific limits from Section 2.6. This guide covers the parts that change how you plan a project. For the barrier's physical specs (height, gaps, gate hardware), see our [pool enclosure page](/fence-styles/pool-enclosure/).

## What needs a barrier

The standards require a security barrier that complies with the **International Residential Code** around any **pool, hot tub, spa, or pond deeper than two feet**. That covers in-ground pools, above-ground pools, spas, and deeper water features, not just the backyard pool most people picture.

A barrier fence also has to meet the regular fence rules in Section 2.6, including height limits, materials, and placement. On a golf course or lake lot, for example, a solid fence isn't allowed along the rear line, so a see-through steel barrier usually does both jobs. Our [height and placement guide](/resources/woodlands-fence-rules-height-placement/) covers those limits.

## Inspections are part of the process

Fences that serve as a pool, spa, or pond barrier need a **code inspection**, on top of the usual written approval from your village's Residential Design Review Committee. The standards call for the inspection to be done by a **third-party qualified inspector**.

That changes the order of work on a new pool. The fence design, the gates, and the hardware all need to be right the first time, because a failed inspection means another trip and another fee.

## What triggers a new inspection

Owners often don't expect this part. Under the standards, you need a new application and a new third-party inspection when:

- **More than eight linear feet** of barrier fencing is replaced, redesigned, reconstructed, or relocated, or
- **Any gate** in the barrier is installed, repaired, or replaced.

So a storm repair that replaces a long run of a barrier fence, or a new gate on the side yard, isn't just a fence job. It reopens the barrier inspection. We plan for that when we quote work on a fence that encloses a pool. Our [repair approval guide](/resources/woodlands-fence-repair-approval/) covers the eight-foot rule for regular fences.

## Shared property-line fences

Many backyards use the property-line fence as the pool barrier. The standards allow it, but they also warn that **selecting a shared property-line fence as the barrier may result in additional code inspections**.

The reason is simple. You and your neighbor both own that fence. If your neighbor replaces a section, your barrier changes too. Some owners avoid that by building a separate barrier closer to the pool, inside their own yard. It costs more up front but keeps the barrier under one owner's control.

An existing fence built after **January 1, 1986** to enclose a pool, spa, or pond on a neighboring lot can count as a barrier under conditions set out in the standards. If your plan relies on an older neighbor's fence, have the Township confirm it before you build around it.

## A temporary barrier while the fence is down

Any time part of the permanent barrier is compromised, removed, or rebuilt, the owner has to put up **temporary security barrier fencing**. That applies during a fence replacement, and it applies after a storm knocks a section down.

When we replace a fence that serves as a pool barrier, we plan the work so the yard isn't left open, and we talk through the temporary barrier before the old fence comes out.

## New pools: the construction barricade

While a pool is being dug and built, the standards require a **safety barricade at least four feet tall** that blocks pedestrian access to the construction area. That's separate from the permanent barrier, and it's usually the pool builder's job. The permanent barrier is what has to pass inspection.

## Above-ground pools

Above-ground pools have one extra rule. The pool, including any external piping, has to be **screened from view by a six-foot solid fence or wall**. On most lots that means a privacy fence around the pool area. The standards also say **pneumatic pool enclosures are not an acceptable pool barrier**.

## Equipment screening

Pool pumps, filters, and above-ground piping have to be screened from view at ground level from any street. A short run of matching fence or a screened enclosure next to the house usually handles it. We can build that at the same time as the barrier.
