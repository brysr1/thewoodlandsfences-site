A gate gets more use than any other part of a fence, so it's usually the first thing to go out of line. The good news: most sagging gates have one of four causes, and you can tell which with a level and a few minutes.

## Step 1: Find the cause

Close the gate and look at the gap between the gate and the latch post, then check each piece in order.

### Is the hinge post leaning?

Put a level on the hinge-side post. If it leans toward the gate, the post is the problem, and everything else is just following it. This is a common cause on older fences, because the gate's weight pulls on that one post every day.

### Is the gate frame racking?

If the post is plumb, check the gate itself. Hold a level or square on the frame. A gate that's out of square, with the far bottom corner drooping, has a frame problem. Wood gates without a good diagonal brace, or with loose joints, rack over time.

### Are the hinges loose?

Grab the latch end and lift. If the gate moves at the hinges, the screws have pulled out of the wood or the hinges are bent.

### Is it only the latch?

If the gate swings freely and looks square but won't catch, the latch and strike may just be out of line, often from a little post movement through the seasons.

## Step 2: Fix it

### Latch out of line

Move the strike or the latch to meet the other. This is the easy one.

### Loose hinges

Replace stripped screws with longer, thicker exterior screws or through-bolts, and replace bent hinges with heavier ones. Undersized hinges on a heavy gate will keep loosening.

### Racked frame

Square the gate up, then hold it there:

- **A diagonal wood brace** works only if it runs the right way: from the **bottom corner on the hinge side up to the top corner on the latch side**, so it pushes the latch corner up.
- **An anti-sag cable kit** with a turnbuckle runs the opposite way, from the **top hinge corner down to the bottom latch corner**, and pulls the latch side up as you tighten it.

If the frame is rotted or the joints have failed, rebuilding the gate is better than bracing it. We build walk gates on steel frames or braced cedar frames to resist sag.

### Leaning hinge post

This one rarely fixes for long with braces or wedges. If the post is loose in the ground or rotted at grade, it needs to be replaced and reset, ideally deeper than a line post. Then re-hang the gate. If the latch post is also failing, replace both.

## Fixing a sagging farm or ranch gate

Long pipe or tube gates on acreage put a lot of weight far from the hinges. When one sags, the hinge post usually goes first: it leans toward the gate or loosens in soft ground, and the latch end drops.

The common fixes:

- **Reset or brace the hinge post.** Dig it out, set it plumb and deeper, or tie it into an H-brace so the next post down the line shares the load.
- **Add a gate wheel.** A wheel under the latch end carries part of the weight, as long as the ground it rolls on is fairly level.
- **Add a diagonal brace** to a frame that has started to bend.

Call a pro if the post is rotted or set in concrete that has cracked, if the gate is wide enough for trailers, or if it has an opener. See our [farm and ranch fencing](/materials/farm-ranch/) page.

## When to call a pro

- The **hinge post is rotted** or loose in the ground.
- The gate is **wide, heavy, or a double drive gate**.
- It's an **automatic gate**. Leave operator adjustments to someone who knows the safety settings. See [gate operators](/fence-styles/gate-operators/).
- It's a **pool gate**. Pool gates must self-close and self-latch, and in The Woodlands, work on a barrier gate needs approval and a new inspection. See our [pool fence rules guide](/resources/woodlands-pool-fence-rules/).
