Walk any street in The Woodlands and count the fences: most of them are shared. The back line and at least one side of a typical lot sit between two yards, so when that cedar starts leaning, it's both neighbors' problem. Replacing a shared fence goes smoothly when the two of you settle a few things before anyone calls a contractor.

This guide covers The Woodlands first, and the same approach works for shared fences in Porter, New Caney, and Splendora, where your HOA or deed restrictions take the Township's place.

## Who has to pay for a shared fence in Texas?

Less than most people expect. Texas has no state statute that makes neighbors split the cost of a fence on the property line. According to the Texas State Law Library, a landowner has no legal obligation to share in the cost or future maintenance of a fence built by a neighbor on the dividing line, **unless they agreed to do so**.

In practice, most neighbors split it anyway, since both yards get a new fence. But it's a conversation, not a bill you can send. If one neighbor pays for the whole fence, Texas courts have generally treated it as that owner's fence, which matters later if it needs repairs.

## What to agree on before you get bids

A five-minute talk over the fence saves a lot of friction later. Settle these points first:

- **The split.** Half and half is common, but a corner lot or a fence that's mostly on one side can justify a different number.
- **The style and height.** Board-on-board, shadowbox, or side-by-side pickets, and whether you're staying at six feet. Both of you will look at it for years.
- **Who signs and who pays the contractor.** One contract with both owners named is cleanest. Put the split in writing, even an email both of you reply to.
- **Stain or no stain.** Decide now whether it gets stained, when, and who covers it.
- **Dogs and pools.** If either yard has a dog or a pool, agree on how the yard stays closed while the old fence is down.

## Where the line actually is

Old fences drift. Posts get reset a few inches off, and a fence from the original builder may not sit exactly on the line. Before you replace a shared fence, pull out the survey from your closing papers and check it against the fence. If the corners can't be found or the two of you disagree, a new survey is worth having. Our [property survey guide](/faq/property-survey/) covers when you need one.

Rebuilding in the same spot is usually the easy answer. Moving a shared fence onto one side of the line is a bigger decision, because it changes who owns it and who maintains it.

## Township approval for a shared fence

The Woodlands' Residential Development Standards don't treat a shared fence differently when it comes to approval. Replacing more than eight linear feet needs written approval from your village's Residential Design Review Committee, and a full run between two yards is well past that. Our [repair approval guide](/resources/woodlands-fence-repair-approval/) explains the eight-foot rule.

### The finished side faces the neighbor

On a new fence, the finished side faces the street or neighboring property. That means one neighbor sees the rails. If you both want a finished face, shadowbox is the simplest way to get it.

### Good-neighbor fences

A "good neighbor" fence alternates sections so each side gets the finished face on some panels. Building a new one needs your neighbor's written consent, but the standards waive that consent when you're replacing an existing good-neighbor fence.

### Pool barriers

If the shared fence is part of a pool barrier, the job carries extra requirements, including a temporary barrier while it's down and an inspection afterward. Our [pool fence rules guide](/resources/woodlands-pool-fence-rules/) walks through them.
