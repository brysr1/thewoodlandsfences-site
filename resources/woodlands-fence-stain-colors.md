Stain is the last step on a new cedar fence, and in The Woodlands it's also a rules question. Section 2.6 L and Appendix C of the Township's [Residential Development Standards](https://www.thewoodlandstownship-tx.gov/files/sharedassets/main/v/2/covenant-administration/documents/2024-residential-standards-recorded-12-10-2024.pdf) set out what you can put on a fence without an application and what needs approval first.

## Option 1: Let it weather

The standards say wood fencing **must be permitted to weather naturally**. In plain terms, you're never required to stain. Cedar left alone turns silver-gray over time.

The trade-off is maintenance, not rules. Under heavy tree cover, bare wood tends to collect mildew and pollen. Our [staining and power washing page](/fence-staining-power-washing/) covers what a stain does for the wood.

## Option 2: Pre-approved stains

**Transparent and semi-transparent stains** shown in Appendix C are **pre-approved**. Appendix C says all fence stains must be transparent or semi-transparent unless otherwise approved, and its examples are natural wood tones, a brown, and a gray, the kind of colors that let the grain show through.

That's the easy path. Restaining in a pre-approved tone doesn't need an application.

## Option 3: Paint, solid stain, or tinted sealant

These are allowed, with conditions:

- The color has to be a **muted shade**.
- It needs **approval before it goes on**.
- **Gloss paint** is on the Township's list of unacceptable materials.

Appendix C notes that reviewers consider the **character of the neighborhood** and how **visible the fence is from the street**, and that screening may be required case by case. A dark solid stain on a backyard run that no one sees from the street is a different request from the same color on a fence along a main road.

Section 1.5 also lists **color changes** among the work that needs a permit, so switching from a natural stain to a solid color is a new approval, even on an existing fence.

## White fences

White fences are considered **only where they're architecturally appropriate**. If you want a white picket fence to match a house's trim, plan on making that case in your application.

## Timing the first coat

New wood needs time to dry before it takes stain well. Stain applied too early can look blotchy and wear unevenly. We'll tell you when a new fence is ready. Our [best time to build guide](/resources/best-time-build-fence-woodlands/) covers how the seasons and pine pollen affect when to stain.

## Matching an existing color

If you're repairing part of a fence, matching the old stain keeps the job a repair rather than a color change. New boards won't match weathered ones exactly at first, so it's often worth cleaning and restaining the whole run. Our [repair approval guide](/resources/woodlands-fence-repair-approval/) covers when a repair needs an application.
