When you compare fence quotes in The Woodlands, the totals are the least useful number on the page. Materials cost about the same for every contractor, so when one bid comes in well under the others, something was left out: depth, rails, hardware, cleanup, or the paperwork. The trick is finding out what.

These are the seven things we'd check on any bid, ours included, for a fence in The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, or Splendora.

## What to check when you compare fence quotes

### 1. Is everyone pricing the same fence?

Before comparing prices, compare the scope. Check the linear footage, the height, the style (side-by-side, board-on-board, or capped), the number and width of gates, and whether a cap or trim is included. A quote that measured 140 feet and one that measured 165 feet aren't competing on price. If one contractor skipped a side yard or priced a single walk gate where you asked for a double drive gate, put the missing pieces back before you decide.

### 2. How deep are the posts, and what happens if the hole is wet?

Posts decide how long a fence stays straight, so ask how deep they go and how much concrete each one gets. Our standard is at least 24 inches and 80 pounds of concrete per post, deeper in loose or wet ground.

Then ask the follow-up most homeowners skip: what do you do when the hole fills with water? On the flatter lots around The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, and Splendora, sandy loam sits over a clay subsoil that comes closer to the surface than it does on higher ground. From fall into spring, water perches on that clay, and post holes slough in or fill up. We pump the water out and set a gravel base before any concrete goes in. A crew that pours into standing water saves an hour and costs you a leaning fence. It's also fair to ask whether the bid uses wood or galvanized steel posts, since that changes both price and lifespan.

### 3. How many rails, and how are they attached?

Two rails let pickets cup and warp between them. Three rails make a stiffer fence. Ask how the rails connect to the posts. We use Simpson brackets, and we fasten pickets with hot-dipped galvanized ring-shank nails so they hold and don't streak rust down the wood. Also look for a rot board. Without one, the bottoms of the pickets sit in damp soil and mulch.

### 4. What's holding up the gate?

Most gate complaints start with the post, not the gate. A heavy gate hung on an ordinary line post will sag within a season or two. Ask whether gate posts are set deeper and stiffer than line posts, what hinges and latch are included, and whether a wide drive gate gets a wheel or extra bracing. If the quote just says "gate," ask for detail.

### 5. Is tear-out and haul-off in the price?

Removing an old fence, breaking out old concrete footings, and hauling it all away takes real labor. Some quotes list it as an extra, and some leave it off and add it on build day. Ours includes it. Also ask what happens if the crew hits buried concrete or a big root mass. The right answer is that they stop and talk with you before the price changes.

### 6. Who handles approval, and does the design pass?

In The Woodlands, nearly every new fence, and any repair that replaces more than eight linear feet, needs written approval from your village's Residential Design Review Committee. Ask who prepares the packet. Then check that the quoted fence can actually be approved. Vinyl and unscreened chain link aren't allowed in The Woodlands, the finished side has to face the street or neighbor, and trees over six inches in diameter can't come out to make room for the fence. A cheaper bid for something your committee will reject isn't cheaper. Our [approval guide](/resources/woodlands-fence-approval-rdrc/) walks through the process.

In Porter and New Caney, most master-planned communities have their own architectural review, and some name a style and stain color. Every contractor should also request Texas 811 utility locates before digging.

### 7. What does the written warranty cover?

"We stand behind our work" isn't a warranty. Ask for it in writing, how long it runs, and what it excludes. Ours is an 18-month workmanship warranty from the day we finish, and the [warranty page](/warranty/) lists what's covered and what isn't. Materials like hardware and metal panels may carry separate manufacturer warranties, so ask about those too.
