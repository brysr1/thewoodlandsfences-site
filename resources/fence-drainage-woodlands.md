After a heavy rain, walk your back line and watch where the water goes. On a lot of yards in The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, and Splendora, it runs straight for the fence and sits there. Fence drainage is one of the things homeowners rarely think about until boards start rotting from the bottom up or a neighbor knocks on the door about a pond in their yard.

A fence can't fix a drainage problem, but a badly built one can make it worse. Here's what we look at on a wet lot, and how we build so the water keeps moving.

## Why water collects along fence lines here

This side of Montgomery County is mostly sandy and fine-sandy loam on top, with a clay and plinthite layer underneath. On the flatter ground around The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, and Splendora, that clay sits closer to the surface. In wet months, water soaks through the sandy layer, hits the clay, and has nowhere to go. That's a perched water table, and it's why some yards stay soggy for days.

Builders also often grade yards to drain toward the side and rear lot lines, which is exactly where the fence goes.

## How a fence can block drainage

### Solid boards down to the dirt

A privacy fence with pickets touching the ground works like a low wall. Water that used to sheet across the yard backs up behind it. Add pine needles, leaves, and mulch packed against the bottom, and the dam gets tighter every season.

### Posts in the path of a swale

Many lots have a shallow swale, a gentle ditch in the grass, that carries water to the street or a drain. A fence built across it, or with a post set in the middle of it, slows that flow down.

### Fill and landscaping against the fence

Beds built up against the fence, or soil added to level a yard, raise the ground on one side. Now water and dirt both sit against wood that was never meant to be buried.

## What standing water does to a fence

- **Picket rot.** The bottom inch of a picket that stays wet will soften and break off first. You'll see a ragged, uneven bottom edge.
- **Post failure.** Wood posts rot at the ground line, where air and water meet. Steel posts handle wet ground better, which is a big part of our [steel vs. wood posts comparison](/resources/steel-vs-wood-fence-posts/).
- **Leaning sections.** Saturated ground holds a post less firmly. A run that's wet half the year can lean after a strong wind.

## How we build a fence that lets the water through

### Leave a gap at the bottom

We hold the pickets a couple of inches off the ground so water and debris can pass under. On a low spot, we may raise that section a little more.

### Use a rot board where it makes sense

A rot board (also called a kickboard) is a horizontal board run along the bottom of the fence. It takes the abuse from moisture and soil instead of the pickets, and it can be replaced on its own. We use ground-contact treated lumber for it. On a lot where water must cross the line, we leave openings at the low point rather than seal the whole run.

### Don't pour concrete into a wet hole

On these lots, post holes often slough in or fill with groundwater as we dig. We never pour concrete into standing water. We get the water out, set a gravel base, then set the post. Concrete poured into a muddy hole doesn't cure right and won't hold.

### Respect swales and easements

We note where water flows when we walk the line, and keep posts out of swales where we can. If the fence crosses a drainage easement, the easement owner has a say. Our [flood zone and easement guide](/faq/flood-zone-easement-fencing/) covers those rules, including the Township's approval requirement inside The Woodlands.

## Your neighbor's yard counts too

Texas Water Code Section 11.086 says no one may divert or impound the natural flow of surface water in a way that damages another person's property. A fence that turns your neighbor's yard into a pond can become more than a friendly disagreement. A gap at the bottom and a clear path for water avoid it.
