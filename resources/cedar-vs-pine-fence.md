Ask three neighbors what their fence is made of and you'll hear cedar, pine, and "I'm not sure." The cedar vs. pine fence question matters more than it seems on a wooded Woodlands lot, where shade, pine needles, and damp ground work on a fence every day. Both woods make a good fence. They just spend your money differently.

We build with both in The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, and Splendora. Here's how pressure-treated pine and cedar compare, one factor at a time, and why the best answer is often a mix.

## What you're actually choosing between

**Pressure-treated pine** is southern yellow pine forced full of preservative under pressure. The treatment is what lets it resist rot and insects. Untreated, pine doesn't last long in our climate.

**Cedar** comes in two common grades for fences around here. Japanese cedar is the popular value choice for backyard privacy fences. Western red cedar is the premium option, with more natural rot resistance and boards that stay flatter. Our [wood and cedar page](/materials/wood-cedar/) goes deeper on all three.

## Cedar vs. pine fence, compared

### Cost

Pine wins on price. On our pricing guide, a pressure-treated pine fence typically runs $25 to $35 per linear foot installed, and cedar runs $30 to $47. The low end of cedar overlaps the high end of pine, which is why so many owners in The Woodlands land on Japanese cedar pickets. Height, style, gates, and trees on the line can move the total as much as the wood does.

### Rot and termites

This one is closer than people expect. Treated pine gets its protection from chemistry, and that protection goes all the way through a properly treated board. Cedar gets its resistance from natural oils in the heartwood, and the lighter sapwood has much less of it. Termites are active across Montgomery County, and any wood touching soil or mulch is fair game. For anything set in the ground, treated pine rated for ground contact or galvanized steel is the right call, not cedar.

### Shrinking, twisting, and gaps

Cedar wins clearly. Treated pine usually arrives wet from treatment, and it shrinks and can twist or cup as it dries through its first year. On a side-by-side fence, that opens gaps between pickets. Cedar moves less, and western red cedar is one of the most stable softwoods you can buy. If you want pine pickets and full privacy, a board-on-board layout hides the shrinkage.

### Looks

Cedar has the warm reddish-brown color most people picture when they think of a nice wood fence. New pine is pale tan or slightly green. Both turn gray in the sun if left bare, and under heavy tree cover both pick up mildew and pollen.

### Staining and Township rules

Here's where The Woodlands changes things. The Township pre-approves transparent and semi-transparent stains, and anything more opaque needs approval. Gloss paint is prohibited outright. Cedar looks great under a semi-transparent stain. Pine can too, but an opaque stain that would hide its color needs approval first, so judge how you like the bare wood before you commit. Pine also needs to dry before it takes stain well, so plan on a wait after install. Our [staining and power washing service](/fence-staining-power-washing/) handles both woods.

### Wet ground and fasteners

Neither wood fixes a bad post hole. On the flatter lots around The Woodlands, Porter, New Caney, and Splendora, clay sits closer to the surface, and in wet months water perches on top of it. Holes slough in or fill with water. We never pour concrete into a wet hole; we pump it out, set a gravel base, then set the post. One more pine detail: modern treated lumber is harder on fasteners, so we use galvanized or stainless hardware rated for it. Plain steel screws will rust.

## Why most good fences use both

The fence most of our customers pick isn't all cedar or all pine. It's **cedar pickets on a treated pine frame**, or cedar pickets on galvanized steel posts. The pickets are what you see, so they get the better wood. The posts and rails do the structural work and live closest to the wet ground, so they get treatment or steel. Our [steel vs. wood posts comparison](/resources/steel-vs-wood-fence-posts/) covers that part of the decision.

## The practical takeaway

- **Choose pine** for the lowest cost per foot, a long run on a back line, or a fence that will be stained right after it dries.
- **Choose cedar** for a privacy fence you'll look at every day, a side-by-side style where gaps would show, or a fence that will wear a semi-transparent stain.
- **Either way,** ask what the posts are, how deep they go, and what happens when a hole fills with water.
